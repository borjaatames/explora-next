Work on the Mosque-Cathedral of Córdoba began in 785, when the Umayyad emir Abd al-Rahman I built a mosque on the site of the Visigothic basilica of San Vicente. Over the next two centuries Abd al-Rahman II, al-Hakam II and Almanzor enlarged it until it became one of the largest mosques in the Islamic world.

Its defining image is the prayer hall: a forest of marble, jasper and granite columns, many of them reused from Roman and Visigothic buildings, carrying double arches with red and white voussoirs. Al-Hakam II's extension in the 10th century added the mihrab, with gold mosaics made with the help of craftsmen sent from Byzantium, and the domed maqsura in front of it.

After Ferdinand III conquered Córdoba in 1236 it was consecrated as a cathedral. In 1523 work began on the Renaissance nave and transept in the middle of the hall, which took almost a century to finish, and the old minaret was encased in today's bell tower. It has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1984, a listing extended to the city's historic centre in 1994.
